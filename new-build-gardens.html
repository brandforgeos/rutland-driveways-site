Questions

Things people ask us

How much does it cost to landscape a new-build garden?
Most new-build gardens are handed over as compacted subsoil under a thin layer of topsoil, with a small slab by the back door. We break up the compaction, sort the drainage, then build the patio, lawn, beds and boundaries; a full new-build garden typically costs £15,000–£35,000.
Why does turf fail on new-build gardens?
Because the ground underneath is compacted subsoil and rubble. Breaking it up and importing proper topsoil is the step that makes a lawn last.
Why is my new-build garden waterlogged?
Usually because site machinery compacted the ground and it was then covered with a thin layer of topsoil, so rain sits on a dense layer it cannot soak through. Buried rubble and heavy clay subsoil make it worse, and on some estates the plot falls towards the house or a neighbour. Breaking up the compacted layer and adding land drains to a proper outfall usually fixes it; extra topsoil on its own rarely does.
Should I wait before landscaping a new-build garden?
There is usually no need to wait a year for the ground to settle, but it is worth waiting until any snagging the developer still owes on drainage, fences or levels is finished, and a wet spell will show you where water sits. Check your deeds and planning conditions first, and keep finished levels at least 150mm below the house's damp-proof course. After that, the sooner the compaction is dealt with, the sooner a lawn can establish.
Are there restrictions on what I can do with a new-build garden?
Often, yes. Many estates have covenants in the deeds or planning conditions that keep front gardens open-plan, limit fence and wall heights or remove some permitted development rights, and a management company may control shared verges. Rear gardens usually have more freedom. Check the deeds, the transfer plan and the estate's planning decision before designing anything that faces the road; we go through them with you before anything is drawn.
Will landscaping affect my new-build home warranty?
Garden landscaping is not usually covered by a new-home warranty, but careless work can affect cover for the house itself. Raising soil or paving to within 150mm of the damp-proof course, blocking air bricks or burying drainage access points can cause damp or drainage problems that a warranty provider may refuse to accept. Keep levels below the damp course, leave inspection chambers reachable and read your warranty terms before altering anything against the house.
How do you improve the soil in a new-build garden?
By breaking up the compacted subsoil to depth, removing buried rubble and then importing screened topsoil, rather than rotavating the thin layer the developer left. Lawns want at least 100–150mm of good topsoil, and beds and borders 300mm or more with organic matter worked in. On heavy clay, grit or sand worked into the surface helps drainage. It is the least visible part of the job and the one that decides whether anything grows.
What should I do first with a new-build garden?
Sort the ground and drainage first, because everything else sits on them: break up the compaction, put land drains in where water sits and set the levels. Then build the hard landscaping, usually a bigger patio than the small slab by the back door and a path or two, followed by fencing or screening, with lawn and planting last. On a tighter budget, doing the groundwork and patio now and turf and planting later works well.
How long does it take to landscape a new-build garden?
A full new-build garden with drainage, patio, lawn, beds and boundaries typically takes two to four weeks on site, after the design and quote. Smaller jobs, such as breaking up the ground and laying a patio and new lawn, are quicker. Prolonged wet weather slows work on the heavy clay found on estates around Oakham and Market Harborough, because saturated ground cannot be worked or turfed without damaging it.
What is the cheapest way to sort out a new-build garden?
Put the budget into the ground and one good outdoor space: break up the compaction, fix the drainage and lay a decent patio and a lawn on proper topsoil, which as a garden refresh typically costs £6,000–£12,000. Fencing, lighting and planting can follow in later phases without undoing anything. Skimping on the groundwork is the false economy, because turf laid on compacted subsoil often struggles from its first summer.
