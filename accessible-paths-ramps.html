Accessible paths & ramps

What it is

We build permanent concrete and paved access ramps, level landings and widened paths so wheelchairs, walkers and pushchairs can get to the door safely, including adaptations funded by a Disabled Facilities Grant.

Accessible paths & ramps

Disabled Facilities Grants

A Disabled Facilities Grant from your local council can pay for adaptations such as ramps, level access and widened paths, up to £30,000 in England, after an occupational therapist has assessed what is needed. Some councils top that up.

We work to the occupational therapist's recommendations and the council's schedule, and we are happy to work directly with council grant officers.

Questions

Things people ask us

How much does an access ramp cost?
A permanent concrete ramp with handrails and a non-slip surface typically costs up to about £2,500. Longer ramps, landings and widened paths are priced on the visit. Disabled Facilities Grant work is priced to the council's schedule.
Can I get a grant for a disabled access ramp?
Possibly. A Disabled Facilities Grant from your council can pay for adaptations such as ramps and level access, up to £30,000 in England, after an occupational therapist's assessment. Some councils top that up.
How steep can a wheelchair access ramp be?
As a guide, no steeper than 1:12 for a short ramp and gentler for a longer one, with level landings. We set it out to suit the person using it.
Do I need planning permission for an access ramp?
An access ramp or level path at a house does not normally need planning permission, but a raised ramp or platform more than 30cm above the ground can. A listed house can need listed building consent for changes to its fabric, and in conservation areas with Article 4 directions, such as Stamford's, road-facing works can need permission. We check the address before the design is fixed.
Does an access ramp need Building Regulations approval?
A ramp or path added outside an existing home does not usually need Building Regulations approval on its own, though it can when it forms part of an extension or other notifiable building work. Approved Document M still sets out sensible gradients, widths and landings, and we build to it. Disabled Facilities Grant work also follows the occupational therapist's specification and the council's process.
How wide should a wheelchair access ramp be?
A wheelchair access ramp at a home should have a clear width of at least 900mm, the minimum Approved Document M gives for approach routes to dwellings, and a little wider is more comfortable for a carer or a larger chair. Level landings at the top and bottom, about 1.2m long and clear of any door swing, give room to stop and open the door safely.
Does an access ramp need handrails?
Most access ramps benefit from handrails, and longer ramps, or ramps used by someone unsteady on their feet, should have one on each side, set at about 900–1,000mm above the ramp surface. A short, gentle ramp for a wheelchair user may not need them, which is why the occupational therapist's report usually decides. Rails should be easy to grip, continuous and run past the top and bottom of the slope.
What is the best non-slip surface for an outdoor ramp?
Brushed concrete is the most common non-slip surface for an outdoor ramp because it grips well wet or dry and is easy to keep clear. Textured paving or resin bound surfacing also work, provided the material is rated for slip resistance and the ramp sheds water. Smooth paving, timber decking and painted surfaces get slippery with algae or frost and are best avoided on ramps.
How long does a Disabled Facilities Grant take?
A Disabled Facilities Grant often takes several months from first contact to work starting: an occupational therapist assesses the need, the council checks eligibility and the quotes, then approves the grant, and it must decide within six months of a complete application. Work should not start before approval or the grant can be refused. The grant is means-tested for adults but not for disabled children.
How long does it take to build a concrete access ramp?
A standard concrete access ramp usually takes a few days on site to dig out, form, pour and finish, with handrails fitted once the concrete has hardened. The concrete then needs a few more days before heavy use, so a safe temporary route to the door has to be thought through. Longer ramps with several landings, or work to widen a path, take longer.
