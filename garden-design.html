Questions

Things people ask us

How much does garden design cost?
We design and build whole gardens in a set order: survey and levels first, then retaining walls, steps and drainage, then surfaces, boundaries, lighting and planting. A full rear garden typically costs £15,000–£35,000 and takes two to four weeks on site.
Can a garden be built in phases?
Yes. We plan the whole garden first, then build it in stages in an order that does not undo earlier work.
Is garden design included with a full garden build?
Yes: for full garden transformations, design is part of the service, and the whole build is priced as one written figure after the site visit. We survey the garden, settle levels, drainage and layout on paper, and you choose materials from samples held against your own walls. A full rear garden typically costs £15,000–£35,000 and takes two to four weeks on site.
What does a garden design include?
For most gardens, a scaled layout plan and a material palette are enough to build from: where each space sits, the levels and steps between them, where water drains, the paving, walling and fencing, and where lighting and planting go. The aim is a plan that can be built on your budget rather than a concept drawing, with the structural decisions settled before anything is dug, while they are still cheap to change.
How long does the garden design stage take?
Usually a couple of weeks for design and quotation, after the first site visit where we measure up and check levels and drainage. Larger or steeply sloping gardens can take longer if levels need surveying in detail or the layout goes through a few versions. Building then typically takes two to four weeks on site for a full rear garden, with the programme set out in the written proposal.
Do I need a garden designer or a landscaper?
If the garden needs its levels, drainage and structure changing, a design-and-build landscaper is often the simpler route, because whoever draws the plan also prices and builds it, so the design stays buildable on your budget. A separate garden designer suits complex planting schemes or a strong design concept, but you then pay for design and build separately and need the two to agree on levels and details.
Where do you start when designing a garden?
Start with how you want to use it, not with materials: where you sit in the morning and evening, how many people you entertain, whether children or dogs need lawn, and how much looking-after you want to do. Then look at the fixed facts: aspect and sun, slopes, drainage, views to hide or keep, and access for machines. The layout follows from those answers, and materials and planting come last.
In what order should a garden be built?
Groundwork, drainage and retaining walls come first, then lighting ducts and the sub-base, then patios, paths and steps, followed by fencing, light fittings, topsoil, turf and planting. Working in that order stops machinery and later trades damaging finished surfaces and new lawns, and means cables and drains sit in ducts under the paving instead of being dug in afterwards. If a build finishes in midsummer, planting is often best left until autumn.
How do you design a long, narrow garden?
Break it into two or three separate spaces rather than one long strip: a terrace by the house, a lawn or planted middle, and a destination at the end such as a seating area or garden room. Paths that run on a diagonal or move from side to side make the garden feel wider, and planting or screens across the width stop you seeing the whole length at once. Check where the evening sun falls before choosing where to sit.
How does the direction a garden faces affect its design?
Aspect decides where the seating goes: a south or west-facing garden gets sun on a terrace by the house, while in a north-facing garden the sunny spot is usually at the far end, so the main seating area often belongs there. Shaded areas suit ferns and woodland planting, and porcelain resists algae better than more porous stone in damp shade. Watch where the sun falls through a summer afternoon before fixing the layout.
How do you design a low-maintenance garden?
Use hard surfaces for the areas you walk and sit on, keep any lawn to a simple shape that is quick to mow, and choose shrubs and perennials that suit the soil so they need little watering or replacing. Mulched beds suppress weeds, raised edges keep borders tidy, and artificial grass can replace lawn in deep shade or dog-worn corners. Low maintenance is not no maintenance: hedges, beds and paving still need an occasional tidy.
